# Tour du Mont Blanc

## Overview
The Tour du Mont Blanc is a loop of about 170 km around the Mont Blanc massif, crossing France, Italy and Switzerland. Most walkers take 10 to 11 days, gaining roughly 10,000 m of cumulative ascent. It is one of the most popular long-distance walks in Europe, so the classic stages are busy in July and August.

## Route
The traditional start is Les Houches in the Chamonix valley, walking anticlockwise. The route climbs to the Col de la Seigne at 2,516 m, the border with Italy, then descends through the Val Veny to Courmayeur. From the Italian Val Ferret it crosses the Grand Col Ferret at 2,537 m into Switzerland. The Swiss section passes La Fouly and the lake at Champex. Strong walkers can take the Fenêtre d'Arpette variant at 2,665 m, the highest point of the tour, instead of the gentler Bovine path. The loop returns to France over the Col de Balme and finishes along the balcony paths above Chamonix, past Lac Blanc.

## When to go
The season runs from mid-June to mid-September, when the refuges are open. Snow can linger on the high cols until early July, and an ice axe is sometimes needed on north-facing slopes in June. September is quieter and often has stable weather, but some huts begin to close in the middle of the month.

## Where to stay
Walkers sleep in mountain refuges, gîtes and village hotels. Refuges serve dinner and breakfast as half board, and many will prepare a packed lunch. Beds in July and August are booked months in advance, so reserving every night before setting out is strongly recommended. Wild camping is restricted in all three countries, especially in Switzerland and on the French side near the Chamonix nature reserves.

## Difficulty
The trail is non-technical but physically demanding: most days include more than 1,000 m of climbing and a long descent. Good fitness and broken-in boots matter more than technical skill. Ladders and short via-ferrata-style sections appear on some variants near Lac Blanc and can be avoided.

## Practicalities
Euros are accepted across the route, although Switzerland uses the franc and some Swiss huts prefer it. Public buses link most valley towns, so stages can be skipped in bad weather. Water is easy to find at refuges and village fountains. A light shell, warm layer and walking poles are standard kit.
